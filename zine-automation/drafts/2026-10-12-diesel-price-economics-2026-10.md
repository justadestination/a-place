# The press is fine. The price of diesel is the press.

The zine runs on printing ink, a bottle that costs about as much as a small car and gets you maybe a year of good work. I spent forty years in a shop that charged paper, labour and wear on the press, and I never once heard an economist call it a "systemic failure." They call it a business model.

So I read the reports about diesel — prices "near doubling in a year," a war half a world away weighting the pump, a president promising "lower prices for Americans" while the figures say the Gulf's cut to supply, driven by the Iran war, is the larger of the two falls. Nothing has changed and everything has.

## What the press says

The framing, as usual, is a fight between two stories. One side says Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries are the cause. The other side, the BBC Verify analysis, says the figures point at the Iran war and its cut to Gulf exports. Both sides have evidence. Both sides are partly right. Neither side mentions the actual cost, what the lorries, the vans, the ships are paying, and what the shop owners, the drivers and the pensioners are paying at the stand.

The government has promised a "regulatory regime" for the heating oil market and to review minimum order levels of 500 litres. Domestic tanks hold between 1,000 and 2,000 litres. The person who gets stuck with it is the one who sells the fuel by the litre and still has to heat the building.

## What I know

A press is a machine. You set the type, you lock the form, you run the press, and you get an impression. You can tell when the ink is right, when the impression is good, and when it's a rumor the paper doesn't want. The press doesn't care about your headline. It cares about the inking, the pressure, the paper.

That's how the diesel market runs. It's a machine in the same way. Crude arrives, a refinery turns it into diesel, a tanker takes it to a port, a lorry takes it to a shop, and a customer pays the price on the pump. Every link adds a charge. Every link is real, just like every ink ribbon and every mangled type character is real. You can't wish the wear away.

The president's promise of lower prices is a headline. The figures are the proof. My inference from the supply numbers, from the cost reports, from the places where the diesel actually stands, is that the supply is what it is, and the price is what it is, and the gap between the two is what politics uses to fill the page.

## What's next

The UK government has promised a cap on gas and electricity tariffs. It has not promised one for heating oil. It has promised a regulatory regime. It has not said when it would take effect.

So the next time someone tells you "the market will fix itself," or "the deal will lower prices," remember the press. It's been around since Gutenberg, and it still doesn't print money. It prints what's set before it, it sets the pressure, and it shows you the impression.

The price of diesel is the press. It's honest. It's immediate. And it never lies about the paper.
