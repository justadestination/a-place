# NEXT YEAR, THE WEATHER WARNING WILL ANSWER BACK

**The Pacific is warming, and the forecasts are getting ugly. Within twelve months, I expect at least three newsrooms or public agencies to offer something unfamiliar: a weather briefing you can question.**

---

I look after the building at night. Mostly this means persuading doors to close and discovering which apparently responsible adult has microwaved fish.

Lately, it also means checking the weather.

Not because I enjoy a coloured map. Because there are rooms below street level, and people in them, and “above-average precipitation” is an oddly upholstered phrase for water coming down the stairs.

The current Pacific forecasts deserve attention. In the NOAA advisory supplied for this article, forecasters put the likelihood of a strong-to-very-strong El Niño continuing through January–March 2027 above 83%. Columbia’s September 21, 2026, model-based outlook reports all 22 participating models forecasting very strong conditions through winter 2026–27.[1][2]

Those forecasts use different measures and methods; their numbers aren’t interchangeable. Nor does an exceptionally strong El Niño guarantee a particular flood, drought, or storm at your address.

But they give us a reason to prepare—and a reason to examine how warnings reach us.

Because a warning can arrive on time and still fail to become something a person can use.

### The bulletin has no idea about the stairs

An alert says *flooding possible*.

You say: “My mother lives downstairs. She can’t manage the steps. What does this warning actually mean for us?”

The alert continues being an alert.

This is the gap I expect more organisations to tackle over the next year: the distance between a published warning and the awkward, specific question somebody needs answered.

**My prediction is that, by October 2027, at least three identifiable newsrooms, meteorological services, or local authorities will publicly launch or pilot conversational weather briefings grounded in current, authoritative information.**

Not a chatbot guessing whether the river looks cross. A service that lets readers ask follow-up questions about a bulletin, see its sources and update time, and find the relevant official guidance.

That is a forecast of adoption, not an announcement of projects already commissioned. My confidence is moderate.

### Three machines, not one miracle

The machinery matters.

**Automation** carries out a workflow: when an official warning is issued, publish it, send a notification, update a page. Traditional rule-based automation is especially good when the conditions and actions are clearly specified.

**Weather models** estimate what the atmosphere or ocean may do. Some use physical equations; some use machine learning; increasingly, systems combine approaches. They are not interchangeable with a conversational language model.

**An LLM** generates language from learned patterns and the context it receives. Connected to a reliable information-retrieval system, it can help explain a bulletin, translate it, or respond to differently phrased questions.

These technologies can work together. Automation delivers the notice. Forecasting supplies the evidence. The language model helps make that evidence accessible.

It should not quietly promote itself to emergency commander.

### Why I think this will happen

A 2025 *Nature Communications* review already identifies risk communication and human–machine interaction as important applications for language models. It also describes personalised warnings and visualisations that make flood impacts more tangible.[3]

That is a research direction, not proof of widespread operational readiness. The same review stresses inaccurate information, bias, uncertainty, and the difficulty of integrating systems into real decisions.

On the publishing side, the Reuters Institute’s 2025 industry survey found 80% of respondents considered AI for personalisation and recommendation important for the year ahead. Its report also describes publishers seeking more direct audience relationships and developing new products.[4]

Those are older signals, not a fresh survey of October 2026. Nevertheless, they help explain the incentive.

A difficult climate outlook creates demand for explanation. Publishers want useful services people return to. Language models offer a way to handle questions that do not fit a dropdown menu.

The pieces are available. Joining them responsibly is the unfinished work.

### What would make the prediction worth welcoming?

Every answer should show where its information came from and when it was updated. Uncertainty should survive the translation into ordinary language. Questions beyond the system’s evidence should lead to a clear admission—not a beautifully furnished invention.

And official evacuation instructions must remain official instructions, not material for improvisation.

If three qualifying services have not appeared by October 2027, this prediction misses. If they appear but invent shelter locations or erase important qualifications, adoption will have happened; progress will not.

I am interested in machine intelligence for a fairly unglamorous reason.

At two in the morning, somebody may need an explanation in another language. Somebody may need the technical phrase unpacked twice. Somebody may have a question the bulletin’s writer never anticipated.

A carefully built system could help without requiring that person to become an expert first.

The forecast is on the screen. The stairs are still there.

Next year, I think more of us will be able to ask about both.
