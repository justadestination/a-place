# THE HOTTEST YEAR OF YOUR LIFE HASN'T HAPPENED YET. IT STARTS IN ABOUT NINETY DAYS.

*A forecast, written in early October 2026, with the receipts attached so you can check it against me in twelve months.*

---

There is a patch of the Pacific Ocean off Peru that is currently running four degrees Celsius hotter than it should be. Not four degrees in the air. Four degrees in the sea, averaged over an area the size of a country, held there for six weeks straight. If your bath did that you'd get out.

That number is not a prediction. It's a measurement, and it is the single most important fact in the world right now that almost nobody is talking about. So let's talk about it, and then I'll tell you what I think it does to the next year of your life.

## What's verified, as of this writing

I want to separate the parts of this piece that are measured from the parts that are me. Here is the measured part, drawn from NOAA's Climate Prediction Center and Columbia's IRI, both updated in the last few weeks:

- The El Niño of 2026 is already **very strong and still strengthening**. The monthly Niño-3.4 index (the standard yardstick) sits at **+2.1°C**; the mid-September weekly reading hit **+3.0°C**. Niño-3 is at +3.0, and Niño-1+2, the coastal Peru box, is at **+3.9°C**.
- Below the surface it's worse. Subsurface water between 50 and 150 metres deep is running **more than 8°C above normal** across the central and eastern Pacific, with pockets exceeding +10°C. That is a reservoir of heat that hasn't reached the surface yet.
- **All 22 models** in the IRI plume, dynamical and statistical alike, agree: very strong El Niño through the winter. The probability of El Niño conditions is **100% through February 2027**. Not 95. A hundred.
- CPC puts the odds at **83%** that the October–December 2026 value will exceed **+2.5°C** on their relative index, which would make this **the strongest El Niño in the entire record back to 1950**. Stronger than 1997–98. Stronger than 2015–16.
- A **positive Indian Ocean Dipole** is forecast to lock in through December, which is the classic co-conspirator that turns an El Niño drought in Australia and Indonesia from bad to historic.
- Separately, before any of this had developed, the WMO already gave an **80% chance** that one of the years 2025–2029 would beat 2024's record of **1.55°C** above pre-industrial, and an **86% chance** that at least one year would exceed 1.5°C.

That's the floor. Everything from here on is me standing on it.

## The prediction

**2027 will be the hottest year ever recorded on Earth, and it won't be close.**

Here's the mechanism, and it's simple enough that you'll be annoyed nobody explained it to you before. The ocean is where the heat is stored. An El Niño is the ocean exhaling that heat into the atmosphere. But the exhale lags the event. The sea surface peaks around December or January; the global air temperature peaks three to six months later. That's why the record years are always the year *after* the El Niño winter: 1998 followed the 1997 peak, 2016 followed the 2015 peak, 2024 followed the 2023 peak.

This event is forecast to peak in December 2026–February 2027, at a strength that may have no precedent in the instrumental record, on top of a baseline that's already roughly a third of a degree warmer than it was during the 1997–98 monster. Stack those and 2027 doesn't just edge past 2024. It jumps.

My specific calls, so they can be graded:

1. **By mid-January 2027**, when CPC publishes the October–December figure, this El Niño will be officially classified as the strongest since 1950. *My confidence: about 80%, which is roughly where NOAA has it, and I see no reason to be braver or more timid than they are.*

2. **At least one month between February and May 2027 will set an all-time monthly global temperature record**, likely by a margin that makes news on its own. *Confidence: 85%.*

3. **The calendar year 2027 will be the warmest on record**, beating 1.55°C. *Confidence: 75%.* I'd put money on it landing somewhere around 1.6 to 1.7°C above the 1850–1900 baseline, though I'll own that specific number as a guess rather than a model output.

4. **Sometime in 2027 a major scientific body will say, in plain language, that 1.5°C is no longer a target but a description.** Not of a single year; of the long-term trend. The WMO's own central estimate for the 2015–2034 twenty-year mean is already 1.44°C. A record 2027 drags that upward. The careful phrasing will be something like "the long-term warming level has reached, or is about to reach, 1.5°C." The headlines will be less careful. *Confidence: 65%.*

## What it does to your groceries

Now the part that touches you whether or not you read climate reports.

The USDA just published its first look at **2027 food prices**, and I want you to look at the shape of their forecast rather than the midpoint. They say food-at-home prices will rise **1.8%**, with a range of **−5.0% to +9.2%**. Food overall: 2.0%, range **−2.7% to +7.0%**.

Read that again. A fourteen-point spread. That is not a forecast; that is a statistical model politely admitting it has no idea, because the thing that will decide the answer, the weather over the next nine months on four continents, hasn't happened yet. The 2026 figure, by contrast, is pinned to a tidy half-point band. The uncertainty for 2027 isn't an accident. It's the El Niño, showing up in a spreadsheet before it shows up in a field.

So here's my second prediction: **the 2027 number will land in the upper half of that band, above 3%, and the pain will be concentrated in the shopping-basket items that El Niño has always hit.** Specifically:

- **Coffee and chocolate.** Retail coffee is already up 6.1% year-on-year; sugar and sweets up 6.6%, driven by chocolate candy. Coffee is grown in exactly the places El Niño dries out (Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia) and cocoa in the places it stresses (West Africa, via the shifted monsoon). Expect both to be a running story through 2027. *Confidence: high.*
- **Rice and palm oil.** Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, India: positive IOD plus El Niño is the recipe for a bad monsoon and a dry Southeast Asia. Watch for at least one major rice exporter to restrict exports by mid-2027, as India did in 2023. *Confidence: 60%.*
- **Fish, in a way you won't see on the label.** The Peruvian anchoveta fishery, the largest single-species fishery on Earth, collapses in strong El Niños because the warm water pushes the fish down and away. Anchoveta becomes fishmeal; fishmeal feeds farmed salmon, shrimp, chickens and pigs. Expect a cancelled or drastically cut Peruvian fishing season and a fishmeal price spike that shows up in protein prices six months later. *Confidence: 75%.*
- **Wheat, already in motion.** US farm-level wheat is up **35.1%** year-on-year on a weak domestic crop. El Niño tends to be *good* for wheat in the US southern plains (wet winter) and *bad* for Australia (drought). Net global effect uncertain, but the volatility is not.
- **Beef, the one place I'll push back on the obvious.** Beef is forecast up 9.4% in 2026, but farm cattle prices fell 6.4% in a single month in August. The herd is small and will stay small; retail beef stays expensive through 2027 regardless of weather. Don't blame El Niño for that one; blame the cattle cycle.
- **Eggs are the good news.** Down 29% this year as flocks recover from bird flu. Enjoy it; it's the only line on this list going the right way.

The macro backdrop is not friendly. The IMF's most recent outlook was titled, with no euphemism at all, "Global Economy in Flux, Prospects Remain Dim." Emerging-market food importers hit by a drought shock in that environment is how you get unrest, and I'll make it a soft prediction that **at least one government falls or faces a serious food-price protest movement in 2027** that is traceable to this event. I won't name the country; I'd just be guessing.

## What the sky does

A quick regional sheet, because strong El Niños are strange but they are not random:

- **Peru and Ecuador**: catastrophic coastal flooding this winter. When Niño-1+2 is at +4°C, the desert gets rain. In 2017 and 1998 this killed hundreds and did billions in damage. *This is my highest-confidence humanitarian call.*
- **East Africa**: floods, not drought, in the short rains, compounded by the positive IOD. Then watch for locust conditions in 2027.
- **Southern Africa, Australia, Indonesia, India's monsoon**: drought and heat. Australia's fire season running into early 2027 is the one to watch.
- **Southern US and California**: a wet, stormy winter. Good for reservoirs, bad for hillsides.
- **Atlantic hurricanes**: El Niño suppresses them. Last year NOAA forecast an above-normal season under neutral conditions; this autumn should finish quiet by comparison, and I expect **the 2026 Atlantic season to end as the quietest since the early 2010s**. The eastern Pacific will be the opposite. *Confidence: 70% on the Atlantic call.*
- **Global coral**: a fourth mass bleaching event confirmed by mid-2027. *Confidence: 80%, and I take no pleasure in it.*

## Where the money will be

One more hook, because it's where the culture is actually going. Prediction markets are now legal, regulated, and enormous in the US: Kalshi did $208 million on a single basketball tournament; Polymarket bought a CFTC-licensed exchange for $112 million; Robinhood and Webull are in; CME and FanDuel are building a platform together. These venues already list contracts on monthly temperatures and hottest-year outcomes.

My prediction: **by spring 2027, "will 2027 be the hottest year on record" will be one of the most-traded non-sports, non-election contracts in the category**, and the odds on it will do the thing scientific reports can't, which is make a line on a chart feel like a bet you've already lost. For a lot of people that market will be the first time climate change has a price with their name on it. I don't think that's entirely a bad thing. Markets are terrible at caring and quite good at noticing.

## How I'd be wrong

Honest forecasters list the exits. Here are mine.

**The event could fizzle.** In 2014 a much-hyped El Niño collapsed. But that one never coupled with the atmosphere; this one has: the Southern Oscillation Index is at −20, the Walker circulation has visibly weakened, the convection has moved east. Fizzling from here would be unprecedented, which is why 22 out of 22 models don't see it. Still, "unprecedented" is a word this decade keeps using up.

**A volcano.** A Pinatubo-scale eruption would throw enough sulfate into the stratosphere to shave a few tenths off global temperature for a year or two. It would also be the single most consequential random event of the decade, so if it happens, you'll have other things to think about than whether I was right.

**A fast La Niña rebound.** Strong El Niños often snap into La Niña by the following summer. IRI already has El Niño odds falling to 61% by mid-2027. A rapid flip could cool the back half of the year and leave 2027 as merely "second warmest." I think the early-year anomalies will be too large to erase, but this is the mechanism I'd bet against me with.

**Food prices could simply not transmit.** Good harvests in the places El Niño favours (the US plains, Argentina) could offset bad ones elsewhere; strong dollar, weak demand, and a dim economy could suppress prices even as supply tightens. That's why USDA's band goes negative. I think the specific commodities I named are exposed enough that their stories hold even if the aggregate number disappoints me.
