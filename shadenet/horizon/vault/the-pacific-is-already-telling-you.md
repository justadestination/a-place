# The Pacific Is Already Telling You Next Year

The prediction markets spent 2026 teaching people to bet on sentences. Logan Sudeith quit a $75,000 finance job after a month in which Kalshi paid him something like $100,000 for guessing what a president would say. Evan Semet built a server to model the same kind of contract. NPR’s Bobby Allyn, in a January 21 piece, found a whole cottage industry of people who had left the office to sit in bed and price the news. The crowd, the pitch goes, knows the future better than the polls.

The crowd is loud. The ocean is louder, and it has already placed its bet.

In mid-September the weekly Niño-3.4 index — the strip of equatorial Pacific the climate agencies actually watch — sat at +3.0°C. Portions of the eastern equatorial Pacific were more than four degrees above average. At depth, between about 50 and 150 meters from 150°W to 80°W, the water was more than 8°C too warm. NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center, in its latest diagnostics discussion, put the chance of a strong-to-very-strong El Niño holding through January–March 2027 above 83 percent. The chance that a three-month value exceeds +2.5°C — stronger than any El Niño in the record back to 1950 — was 83 percent for October–December and still 70 percent for November–January. Columbia’s International Research Institute checked 22 models, dynamical and statistical. All 22 said “very strong” from this autumn through the heart of winter. El Niño probability in that plume does not drop below certainty until after February 2027.

That is not a vibe. That is a loaded ocean.

**Here is the prediction, dated and narrow, so it can be wrong in public.**

By the time the next ENSO discussion cycle has run its course — call it the twelve months from this autumn through next autumn — the 2026–27 El Niño will be entered in the books as one of the two or three strongest events since 1950, and 2026 will finish as the warmest year in the instrumental record, or so close to it that the argument will be about tenths, not about whether the Pacific did the work. The WMO’s annual-to-decadal update already put an 80 percent chance on at least one year between 2025 and 2029 beating 2024, the current record, and an 86 percent chance that at least one of those years clears 1.5°C above the 1850–1900 average. A Niño-3.4 winter at +2.5°C or more is exactly the kind of shove that makes the high end of that range show up early, not in 2029.

The regional handwriting is older than the models, and a coupled event this large raises the odds of the classic pattern even though CPC is careful to say the impacts are more likely, not guaranteed. Through the coming winter and into early 2027, expect the wet side of the El Niño map to collect: heavier rain along the South American coast, where Niño-1+2 has already spent six weeks above +4°C, and a wetter-than-average southern tier of the United States. Expect the dry side to collect too. Convection is already suppressed over Indonesia; the Southern Oscillation Index was −20.1 on the latest 30-day reading, which is the Walker circulation sitting down. Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and northern Australia are the places a very strong El Niño usually invoices for drought and fire. The Indian Ocean is lining up to help. IRI’s September outlook has the Dipole Mode Index leaving a neutral August (+0.2°C) for a strongly positive Indian Ocean Dipole through December — the co-occurrence these two modes are known for — before both start to fade toward neutral in early 2027.

Then the fade. Every model in the plume keeps El Niño alive into spring 2027; the probability only breaks, to 61 percent by mid-2027, in the season when strong events usually collapse. So the prediction has a second half. The damage and the records are a winter story. The argument about whether it was “the strongest ever” is a spring story. By June 2027 the index will be falling, the prediction markets will have moved on to some other sentence, and the people who spent the winter saying the ocean was overhyped will be explaining why the hype and the water level were, for once, the same number.

A note on what this is not. It is not a bet that every El Niño impact arrives on schedule. CPC’s own language is the right one: with an event of this magnitude the chances of the usual impacts are larger, not certain. It is not a claim that a single year above 1.5°C rewrites the Paris Agreement, which is scored on a long average; the WMO was plain that temporary breaches get more common as the baseline climbs, and the central estimate for the 2015–2034 twenty-year mean is already 1.44°C. It is not investment advice for the man in Atlanta. The wallets that “miraculously” nail every OpenAI release date are a different kind of informed money. The Pacific does not trade under a pseudonym.

What the Pacific does is leave a receipt. Subsurface heat above +8°C is not a headline. It is next year’s surface, still underwater, already on its way east. The looms and the language models can argue about what a mind is. This one is simpler. Twenty-two models, one ocean, no card to punch. If you want a number to check me against, write this down: Niño-3.4 at or above +2.5°C for a three-month season sometime between now and January, and 2026 in the top slot of the temperature record when the year is closed. If both miss, the article failed. If only the poetry misses, the ocean didn’t.
